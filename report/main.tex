\begin{abstract}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
We study procedural mistake detection on CaptainCook4D in two settings: step-level
error recognition, and recipe-level task verification against an annotated task
graph. For the first we reproduce the V1 (MLP) and V2 (Transformer) baselines,
add a bidirectional LSTM, extend the pipeline to EgoVLP features, and break the
task down by error category. For the second we build the full four-stage pipeline
the assignment describes: an ActionFormer step localizer trained on our own EgoVLP
features, sequence baselines over step embeddings, Hungarian matching of detected
steps to task-graph nodes through the EgoVLP text tower, and a graph classifier
over the realized graph.

Our central result is negative. Under annotated boundaries the graph classifier
reaches 82.3\% AUC, but all three models fall within \textbf{2.6 AUC of each
other}, less than the 3.3 points DAGNN alone moves across three runs of the
identical configuration and seed, and the best of the three changes between runs.
This holds for DAGNN, the layer designed for directed acyclic graphs, and for a
no-edge pooling control that never reads an edge. A
deterministic rule with no features and no training, ``predict incorrect when a
canonical step is absent'', matches all three on balanced accuracy.

Four independent experiments locate the cause. The pipeline is reading how many
steps were performed rather than how they were structured. Removing every omission
error from the test set collapses all three models by \textbf{20--29 AUC}, two of
them to chance. Swapping annotated boundaries for predicted ones costs the graph
models 11--16 AUC while costing the sequence models nothing, and we trace that asymmetry
to a measured property of the detector. Its boundary quality degrades far faster
out of sample (mean best tIoU $0.891 \rightarrow 0.520$) than its step count
does.
\end{abstract}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Procedural activities --- cooking a recipe, assembling furniture, running a lab
protocol --- are defined less by their individual actions than by the constraints
between them. Some steps must precede others, some may be reordered freely, and
some may be omitted without consequence. A system that understands a procedure
should therefore be able to say not only \emph{what} happened but whether what
happened constitutes a valid execution.

CaptainCook4D~\cite{peddi2024captaincook4d} makes this measurable. It provides 384
egocentric recordings of 24 recipes, 5{,}700 annotated steps of which 1{,}964
(34.5\%) contain at least one error, a taxonomy of eight error types, and --- the
part that matters most here --- a hand-authored task graph per recipe encoding the
partial order over that recipe's steps.

This report covers two tasks. The first is \textbf{supervised error recognition}:
given the video segment of one step, classify it as correct or incorrect. The
second is \textbf{task verification}: given a whole recipe video and the recipe's
task graph, decide whether the execution as a whole was correct. Task verification
is the more useful formulation and the cheaper one to supervise, since it needs
only one binary label per recording rather than one per step.

We implement the four-stage pipeline the assignment prescribes
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) and report it honestly, including where it does not
work. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0.15em
\item \textbf{Baselines and a new backbone.} We reproduce V1/V2 on Omnivore and
SlowFast, add an LSTM variant, extend feature extraction to
EgoVLP~\cite{lin2022egovlp}, and analyse performance per error category, which
shows that F1 and AUC \emph{disagree systematically} on rare categories.

\item \textbf{A step localizer trained on our own features.} We train
ActionFormer~\cite{zhang2022actionformer} on our 1-second EgoVLP features rather
than using published predictions, which lets us evaluate all 384 recordings under
a proper split with post-processing selected on validation alone.

\item \textbf{An analysis of what the classifier uses.} Four experiments --- a
hand rule, a node-feature ablation, a boundary swap and an omission-free subset
--- locate the signal driving recipe-level verification, run over the whole model
roster and including DAGNN~\cite{thost2021dagnn}, the layer built for this
structure.

\item \textbf{A measured account of why detector boundaries hurt the graph but not
the sequence models}, grounded in a train/test generalization gap we quantify per
split.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Mistake detection in procedural video.}
CaptainCook4D~\cite{peddi2024captaincook4d} introduced the benchmark we use, along
with the V1/V2/V3 baselines for supervised error recognition. PREGO~\cite{flaborea2024prego}
addresses the online variant, predicting the next expected step from a symbolic
model and flagging a mistake when the observation disagrees.
HiERO~\cite{peirone2025hiero} learns a hierarchy of activity representations from
egocentric video and shows that reasoning over that hierarchy improves downstream
procedural tasks. It also provides a route to step segmentation without step
supervision, an alternative to the supervised localizer we use in Substep 1.

\paragraph{Task graphs.}
Task graphs compactly encode which orderings of a procedure are valid. They can be
annotated, as in CaptainCook4D, or learned from demonstrations. Differentiable
Task Graph Learning~\cite{seminara2024taskgraph} makes the graph itself the object
of optimisation, and procedure learning from unlabelled egocentric
video~\cite{bansal2022myview, chowdhury2024opel} recovers key-steps and their
order without annotation. We use the dataset's annotated graphs throughout, so our
results isolate what a \emph{correct} graph contributes.

\paragraph{Video backbones.}
Omnivore~\cite{girdhar2022omnivore} and SlowFast~\cite{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}
are the two backbones released with CaptainCook4D features. We add
EgoVLP~\cite{lin2022egovlp}, an egocentric video--language model whose video and
text towers share an embedding space. That shared space is what makes Substep 3
possible at all: it lets a detected step be compared directly against the textual
description of a task-graph node. PerceptionEncoder~\cite{bolya2025perception} and
ImageBind~\cite{girdhar2023imagebind} share the property.

\paragraph{Graph neural networks on DAGs.}
GCN~\cite{kipf2017gcn}, GAT~\cite{velickovic2018gat} and
GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017graphsage} update every node simultaneously from its
immediate neighbours and are agnostic to any ordering the graph encodes. DAGNN~\cite{thost2021dagnn}
instead processes nodes in topological order, so a node updates only after all of
its predecessors have updated within the same layer and a single layer propagates
along an entire path. It is the natural choice for task graphs, which is why we
treat it as the model to beat rather than one option among many. Stacking
neighbourhood-averaging layers is known to over-smooth~\cite{li2018deeper}, which
we test directly.

\subsection{Step-level error recognition}

\paragraph{Baselines.} V1 pools the sub-segment features of a step with an MLP
head. V2 combines them with a Transformer encoder layer before classification. We
keep the official hyperparameters (\texttt{pos\_weight} 2.5, weight decay
$10^{-3}$, batch size 32, decision threshold 0.6 on the \emph{step} split and 0.5
on \emph{recordings}) so the comparison against the published numbers is
meaningful. As a third baseline we add a \textbf{bidirectional LSTM} (hidden 512)
over the sub-segment sequence, which the assignment suggests as a natural
alternative to attention.

\paragraph{A new backbone.} We extend the feature extraction code to
EgoVLP~\cite{lin2022egovlp}, producing $256$-d features. The time base is
load-bearing: the dataloader indexes feature rows with raw annotation timestamps in
seconds, so any stride other than 1\,s silently yields empty slices for the later
steps of a recording. We assert the time base rather than substituting a default.

\subsection{Substep 1: step localization}

We train ActionFormer~\cite{zhang2022actionformer} on our own EgoVLP features,
using the official recordings split (213 train / 62 validation / 109 test) over
353 step classes, with the configuration's frame rate set to match the 1\,s
feature rate. A single checkpoint is selected and used for every downstream
result.

Raw ActionFormer output is dense. We post-process with a score threshold,
class-agnostic temporal NMS and a minimum-duration filter, selecting all three
\textbf{on the validation split only}. The choice is then frozen and the test
split scored once. Each detected segment becomes a step embedding by mean-pooling
the feature rows inside its boundaries.

We export both annotated and predicted boundaries for all 384 recordings and run
every downstream substep on both. The annotation lists only the steps actually
performed, so it supplies the step count directly rather than requiring a model to
infer it; comparing the two sources on identical recordings and labels isolates
that effect.

\subsection{Substep 2: sequence verification baselines}

Three models --- MLP, Transformer and bidirectional LSTM --- consume the sequence
of step embeddings for a recording and emit one binary label. They have no access
to the task graph, and exist to measure how much of Substep 4's performance
requires a graph at all.

\subsection{Substep 3: task-graph encoding and matching}

The 24 annotated task graphs contain 353 nodes and 369 edges in total. A linear
chain over the same nodes would have 329, so the graphs encode genuine branching
rather than a relabelled sequence. Node descriptions are encoded with the
\emph{EgoVLP text tower}, which shares an embedding space with the video tower
that produced the step embeddings. Detected steps are then matched to nodes with
the Hungarian algorithm on cosine similarity, one node per step at most.

The substep assumes the two towers are aligned, so we measure that alignment
directly (\S\ref{sec:matching}).

Matching alone discards execution order: visual features are written onto canonical
nodes and the canonical edges never change, so ``A then B'' and ``B then A'' realize
identically. Since Order Error is the dataset's largest tag (795 instances), each
matched node additionally carries its position in the observed sequence, its segment
boundaries, and a count of canonical edges its position contradicts.

\subsection{Substep 4: classifying the realized graph}

We compare three models chosen to span a specific axis rather than to fill a
leaderboard:

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0.15em
\item \textbf{SimplePooling} --- identical node features, \emph{no edges at all}.
The control.
\item \textbf{GraphSAGE}~\cite{hamilton2017graphsage} --- conventional
neighbourhood message passing, keeping a node's own transformed state separate from
the neighbour aggregate.
\item \textbf{DAGNN}~\cite{thost2021dagnn} --- topological-order processing with
attention over predecessors and a GRU update. The only model that consumes edge
\emph{direction}.
\end{itemize}

The learnable projection the assignment asks for lives here rather than in Substep
3, because it can only be trained where there is a loss: a projector instantiated
in Substep 3 would apply a fixed random transform forever. It fuses the visual and
text blocks and passes the order channels through unchanged.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Recipe-level verification has 384 samples across 24 recipes, so protocol choices
dominate. We use \textbf{nested leave-one-recipe-out}: one recipe is held out for
test, four more are held out from the remaining 23 for epoch selection, and no
metric is ever selected on the test fold. Validation is split \emph{by recipe},
because recordings of the same recipe are near-duplicates and splitting by
recording would make early stopping optimistic.

We report \textbf{pooled} metrics --- all fold predictions concatenated and scored
once --- rather than a mean over folds, since a fold holds 3--6 recordings and its
F1 can take only a handful of discrete values. Per-fold spread is kept as a
dispersion diagnostic.

Finally, 57.29\% of recordings are labelled incorrect, so a constant ``incorrect''
predictor already scores 72.8 F1. \textbf{We read balanced accuracy and AUC}, and
every table carries the majority row.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published baselines.} For Omnivore on the step
split the CaptainCook4D paper reports MLP F1 24.26 and Transformer F1 55.39. We
measure 55.50 and 44.19, approximately the two models exchanged. The AUCs are much
closer, 0.816 and 0.708 against 0.757 for both. We report our own measurements and
treat the published F1 column as not reproduced. The most likely cause is the
operating point. Our MLP sits at 68.64\% precision and 46.59\% recall, and a
different decision threshold moves F1 a long way while barely moving AUC.

\paragraph{The LSTM variant.} No F1 ordering here is meaningful, because the
Omnivore runs are not converged: Omnivore/step moves from 59.64 to 49.64 F1 between
its final two epochs while its AUC moves 0.006, so any single-epoch Omnivore F1 is
a sample from a 7--10 point band. On AUC the MLP leads on both splits, and that is
the ordering we report. With 3{,}752 training steps and features already pooled per
step, little sequential structure remains for recurrence to exploit.

\paragraph{SlowFast's operating point.} SlowFast reaches the two highest F1 scores
in the table at accuracies of 47.62\% and 48.44\% --- below chance on a
34.5\%-positive task --- with recall of 87.55\% and 89.63\%. It predicts positive
almost everywhere and has the lowest AUC of the three backbones. The F1 column
alone would rank it first.

\paragraph{The EgoVLP backbone.} Our EgoVLP features reach 0.698 and 0.700 AUC on
the step split against Omnivore's 0.816. Omnivore features were released with the
dataset and extracted with the authors' own pipeline, whereas ours are a
re-implementation, so this ordering is expected. EgoVLP is used downstream not for
its error-recognition accuracy but for its shared video--text space, which the
other two backbones do not provide.

Training one binary classifier per error tag turns the task from a 34.5\%-positive
problem into a 1--9\%-positive one. Across the twenty runs (5 categories $\times$ 2
variants $\times$ 2 splits) F1 ranges 0.00--38.27 while AUC ranges 0.578--0.916,
and seven of the twenty score below 25 F1 while exceeding 0.75 AUC.
Figure~\ref{fig:category} shows both metrics for both variants on both splits,
with categories ordered by tag count. F1 rises with the base rate and AUC does
not.

TemperatureError is the extreme case, with 66 positives in 5{,}700 steps, F1
exactly 0.00 on both step-split variants, and AUC 0.739 and 0.702. The models order
the steps correctly and never cross the threshold, which is all a fixed 0.6 can
produce on a 1.2\%-positive task.

The two metrics therefore give near-opposite difficulty rankings. By mean AUC the
order is Timing (0.821), Temperature (0.813), Measurement (0.803), Technique
(0.668), Preparation (0.638); by mean F1 it is close to reversed. Our
pre-registered hypothesis, that TimingError would stand out because timing mistakes
have a temporal signature surviving pooling, is confirmed on AUC and refuted on F1:
first of five by AUC on only 177 tags, third by F1. We take the AUC ranking to be
the informative one.

Two caveats belong with this. Rare categories carry wide error bars ---
TemperatureError has roughly 8 positive test steps, so one flipped prediction moves
F1 several points --- and Order Error, the dataset's largest tag, has no
per-category run at all, because ordering is not a property of a single step. That
gap is what the task-graph formulation addresses.

\subsection{Substep 1: localization quality}
\label{sec:loc}

Validation mAP over the 353 step classes averages 36.41\%. Post-processing selected
on validation (score $0.14$, NMS tIoU $0.20$, minimum duration $5.0$\,s) gives, on
the 109 held-out test recordings, segment-level F1 of $0.719$ at tIoU$\geq$0.3,
$0.617$ at $0.5$ and $0.411$ at $0.7$.

The measurement that matters downstream is how these degrade out of sample. Mean
best tIoU --- the tIoU of the best-overlapping prediction, averaged over annotated
segments --- falls from 0.891 on the 213 training recordings to 0.539 on the 62
validation and 0.520 on the 109 test recordings, a factor of $1.7$, while count MAE
rises only from 1.92 to 2.81 to 3.28 and the step-count correlation follows at
0.930, 0.725 and 0.620. Boundary quality and step-count quality therefore degrade at
substantially different rates. Validation reproduces the test figure even though
post-processing was tuned on validation, so this is generalization rather than a
test-split peculiarity.

The direction of the count error also reverses. In sample the detector
over-segments, at $+1.70$ steps; out of sample it under-segments, at $-1.03$ on
validation and $-1.42$ on test, with 65 of 109 test recordings under-segmented
against 31 over-segmented. An under-segmenting detector produces realized graphs
with missing nodes, which \S\ref{sec:shortcut} shows to be the signal the pipeline
uses.

\subsection{Substep 3: matching quality}
\label{sec:matching}

Everything downstream depends on EgoVLP's video and text towers occupying the same
space, so we measure it. Over 100 recordings and 1{,}410 step pairs, video-to-text
retrieval of a step's own description reaches \textbf{26.17\% top-1 against a
7.09\% chance rate}, with correct pairs scoring $0.328$ cosine against $0.221$ for
mismatched pairs (margin $+0.107$). The alignment is therefore real at
$3.7\times$ chance, and weak enough to bound what the matching can contribute.

The match ratio is \emph{not} a measure of matching quality --- Hungarian pads the
cost matrix and always returns $\min(\text{steps}, \text{nodes})$ pairs --- so we
score the assignment directly. Over 5{,}358 assignments on 384 recordings,
\textbf{Hungarian assignment accuracy is 35.91\%}, against 27.12\% for
unconstrained top-1 retrieval and 7.46\% chance. The one-to-one constraint helps,
but roughly two matches in three are still wrong, and every later substep inherits
that error.

One case illustrates the failure mode: in recording \texttt{1\_19} the step
\emph{``Microwave just until cheese melts''} matched \emph{``Continue to Microwave
for 15-30 more sec''} at $0.615$ cosine, above the $0.521$ of the exact
string-to-itself match. Near-duplicate steps within a recipe bound any encoder's
accuracy here.

Mean match similarity does not separate correct from incorrect executions, at 0.367
against 0.374 and in the wrong direction. This follows from the error taxonomy
rather than indicating a defect: CaptainCook4D errors are mostly wrong temperature,
wrong quantity or a skipped step, none of which change how much a \emph{performed}
step resembles its description. What remains usable is the per-node features and the
unmatched-node mask.

\paragraph{Annotated boundaries.} The three models span 81.1, 79.7 and 82.3 AUC,
a range of 2.6 points. Repeating the identical configuration and seed moved DAGNN
alone across 79.0, 81.3 and 82.3 over three runs, a range of 3.3, so the spread
between models is smaller than the variation within one of them. The best of the
three also changes between runs, from the no-edge control to DAGNN. No ranking can
be read from this column. A
no-edge pooling control, a conventional message-passing layer and a layer built
for directed acyclic graphs are indistinguishable on this data, and message
passing over the task graph therefore contributes nothing measurable.

\paragraph{Predicted boundaries.} Here a ranking does appear. SimplePooling leads
both graph models by 3.6 AUC and by 3.0 and 4.3 balanced accuracy. Each margin
alone sits close to the noise floor, but the ordering held in all three runs, with
the no-edge control ahead by 3.1--4.6 AUC every time. Replication rather than
margin is what makes it readable: consuming the graph costs accuracy once the
boundaries are predicted.

\paragraph{The deterministic rule.} ``Predict incorrect when a canonical step is
absent from the match'' reaches 79.1 AUC and 79.0 balanced accuracy using no video,
no encoder, no graph and no training --- above all three learned models on balanced
accuracy, which score 75.7, 76.6 and 76.6. In total 130 of the 384 recordings are
missing at least one canonical step. The rule is deterministic, so it has no folds
and no variance.

\subsection{Locating the signal}
\label{sec:shortcut}

Hungarian matching assigns $\min(\text{steps},\text{nodes})$ pairs, so the
fraction of matched nodes equals the fraction of the recipe's canonical steps that
the recording contains. CaptainCook4D's taxonomy includes a \textbf{Missing Step}
tag with 285 occurrences, and recordings carrying that error are identifiable by
counting alone. The feature is available at test time, so this is a shortcut
admitted by the data rather than a train/test leak. Four experiments test whether
it accounts for the scores in Table~\ref{tab:sub4}.

\paragraph{(i) A rule with no parameters.} The rule above wins the
balanced-accuracy column outright, against 75.7--76.6 for the learned models.

\paragraph{(ii) Node-feature ablation.} Replacing all node features with only the
matched/unmatched mask gives 79.0 balanced accuracy and 77.3 AUC, against 76.8 and
80.2 for the full feature set. Removing all 512 dimensions of visual and text
content therefore costs 2.9 AUC and gains 2.2 balanced accuracy. Removing the count
channels instead (\emph{no\_count}) gives 75.9 and 82.9, and removing the order
channels (\emph{no\_order}) 77.9 and 80.6. Neither removal hurts, because the
matched/unmatched mask still carries the count those channels summarised.

\paragraph{(iii) Boundary source.} Substep 1 exports both sources for the same 384
recordings with the same labels, making this a controlled comparison. Moving from
annotated to predicted boundaries costs the graph models \textbf{10.9, 13.1 and
15.7 AUC} for SimplePooling, GraphSAGE and DAGNN, while the sequence models of
Substep 2 are unaffected or slightly better (Table~\ref{tab:sub2}). The cost again
orders by how much of the graph each model consumes.

A sequence model never computes how many canonical steps are present, so it neither
gains from annotated boundaries nor suffers without them. The graph pipeline does
compute it, and \S\ref{sec:loc} supplies the mechanism: the detector under-segments
out of sample, and to the matcher an under-segmented recording is indistinguishable
from one with a skipped step.

\paragraph{(iv) The omission-free subset.} Restricting to the \textbf{244
recordings with no Missing Step error} removes the shortcut by construction. Every
remaining positive is an Order, Technique, Preparation, Measurement, Timing or
Temperature error, none of which change how many steps were performed. The subset
is 32.8\% positive and the hand rule falls to 52.2\% AUC on it, which confirms it
is built correctly.

\paragraph{Summary.} Recipe-level error detection on CaptainCook4D reduces, in this
pipeline, to whether every canonical step occurred. The EgoVLP embeddings, the text
encoder, the Hungarian matching and the graph structure add little measurable
beyond that, under both boundary sources and with the dataset's annotated
topology.

\subsection{Ablations}

\paragraph{The learnable projection.} With identical folds and seeds, replacing
the fixed $0.5/0.5$ average of visual and text features with the learnable
projection the assignment specifies moves GraphSAGE from 69.64 to 75.88 balanced
accuracy and from 75.37 to 80.25 AUC, gains of $+6.24$ and $+4.88$. Both clear the
noise floor. F1 is the more volatile column at this fold count and should not be
quoted alone.

\paragraph{Depth.} Over 2, 4, 6 and 8 layers GraphSAGE scores 81.50, 77.75, 76.17
and 73.60 AUC, falling monotonically for a total of $-7.90$. This is consistent with
over-smoothing on graphs of 12--14 nodes, where two layers already span most of
the diameter. GraphSAGE is the only model in the roster for which the study is
defined and affordable. SimplePooling stacks no message-passing layers to begin
with, so it has no depth to vary, and DAGNN's runtime scales with layers $\times$
topological depth, which at depth 8 on graphs 19 levels deep is prohibitive.

\paragraph{Edge direction.} Symmetrising the DAG's edges changes GraphSAGE by
$-0.6$ AUC, from 81.2 directed to 80.6 undirected, on 15.3 versus 30.6 edges per
graph. Because GraphSAGE keeps a separate self-pathway, insensitivity is the
expected outcome for this layer, so we report it as a control confirming the
symmetrisation choice does not drive the main table. Whether procedural order is
usable at all is the separate question DAGNN answers in Table~\ref{tab:sub4}.

\paragraph{Run-to-run noise.} \label{sec:noise}
A nine-setting sweep over learning rate and hidden size spans 6.9 AUC points
(77.3--84.1). Separately, re-running the identical configuration and seed moves the
pooled AUC, because the sparse scatter operations in the convolutions are not
deterministic on GPU: summation over a node's incoming edges is ordered by CUDA
atomics, so gradients differ in the last bits and the runs diverge. The drift tracks
how much scatter a model performs. Across three runs SimplePooling, which never
scatters over edges, spanned 0.3 AUC (80.8, 80.9, 81.1); GraphSAGE spanned 1.0
(79.6, 80.6, 79.7); and DAGNN, one attention-plus-GRU sweep per topological level,
spanned 3.3 (79.0, 81.3, 82.3). We therefore treat \textbf{$\approx$3 AUC points} as
the floor below which differences should not be interpreted, and prefer orderings
that replicate across runs to margins that clear it once. The reported configuration
(lr $10^{-3}$, hidden 128) was fixed before any test fold was scored and is not the
best of the nine.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Sources of the negative result.} Three factors compound. The matching is
weak, at 35.91\% assignment accuracy, so roughly two node assignments in three are
wrong and the features written onto the graph are largely misplaced. The graphs are
small, about 13 nodes and 15 edges each, leaving little topology to exploit. Most
fundamentally, the label is dominated by an error type, Missing Step, fully
determined by node presence, which a pooling model reads without edges.

\paragraph{Scope of the claim.} We do not conclude that procedural structure is
uninformative in general, only that this pipeline on this dataset extracts almost
all of its signal from step presence. A stronger matcher, or a label distribution
not dominated by omissions, could change the answer, and the order-violation
statistics suggest the raw material is present: 39.3\% of realized edges are
violated under annotated boundaries, and 98.4\% of recordings violate at least
one.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (a) The omission-free confirmation runs on 244 of 384
recordings, so its folds are smaller and noisier than the main table's, though the
20--29 point drop it measures is an order of magnitude larger than that noise.
(b) All recipe-level numbers rest on 384 samples with a 3-point noise floor, and
the differences we call real clear it by modest margins. (c) Our EgoVLP features
are a re-implementation whose error-recognition AUC sits below the released
Omnivore features, so a better video--text model would raise the matching accuracy
that bounds Substep 3. (d) We report a single seed per configuration, with repeated
runs used only to measure the noise floor.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We built the full task-verification pipeline for CaptainCook4D --- ActionFormer
localization on our own EgoVLP features, sequence baselines, EgoVLP-text task-graph
encoding with Hungarian matching, and a graph classifier --- and evaluated it under
a protocol that selects nothing on the test fold.

Our main finding is that a graph classifier reaching 82.3\% AUC on this benchmark
is largely counting steps. A no-edge control, a conventional message-passing layer
and a layer built for directed acyclic graphs land within 2.6 AUC of one another,
less than one of them moves between runs; a zero-parameter rule beats all three on
balanced accuracy; and removing the one error type that changes the step count
drops every model by 20--29 points.

Two consequences follow. Any task-verification result on CaptainCook4D should be
reported against a no-edge control and against the step-count rule, since both are
cheap and either can account for most of a headline score. And the errors that
remain once omissions are excluded --- wrong temperature, wrong quantity, wrong
technique --- are not visible in pooled one-second clip embeddings. Progress on them
will need finer temporal resolution and object-state reasoning rather than a larger
graph model, while the benchmark's own order-violation statistics indicate the
structural signal is there for a method able to use it.
